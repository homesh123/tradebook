Trading performance metrics explained, with formulas and examples

Updated October 31, 2026

Short answer: The trading metrics that matter most are expectancy (average profit per trade), profit factor (gross profit ÷ gross loss), R-multiple (result measured in units of risk) and maximum drawdown (largest peak-to-trough fall). Together they tell you whether you have an edge, how big it is and how painful it is to trade.

Metrics, formulas and examples

Win rate

The percentage of closed trades that made money.

Win rate = winning trades ÷ closed trades × 100

Example: 17 winners out of 30 trades = 56.7% win rate. A high win rate alone does not mean profitability – it depends on how big wins are compared with losses.

Profit factor

Gross profit divided by gross loss. Above 1.0 is profitable; 1.5 is solid; 2.0 or more is excellent.

Profit factor = sum of winning trades ÷ |sum of losing trades|

Example: $3,000 of wins and $1,500 of losses gives a profit factor of 2.0.

Expectancy

The average amount you can expect to win or lose per trade. Positive expectancy means you have an edge.

Expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss)

Example: 55% × $165 − 45% × $61 = $63.30 per trade.

R-multiple

The result of a trade measured in units of the risk you planned (R). +2R means you made twice what you risked; −1R is a full planned loss.

R = (exit − entry) ÷ (entry − stop loss), reversed for short trades

Example: Long at 100, stop 98 (risk 2), exit 106: R = 6 ÷ 2 = +3R. A loss bigger than −1R means the stop was moved, ignored or slipped.

Risk:reward ratio (planned R:R)

Distance to your target compared with distance to your stop, decided before entry.

R:R = |target − entry| ÷ |entry − stop|

Example: Entry 50, stop 49, target 53 = 1:3. With 1:3 you are profitable even at a 30% win rate.

Average win / average loss

How large winning trades are relative to losing trades.

Payoff ratio = average win ÷ average loss

Example: $165 average win ÷ $61 average loss = 2.7×.

Maximum drawdown

The largest drop of your equity from a peak to a later low. It measures how painful your strategy is to trade.

Max drawdown = max(peak equity − subsequent equity)

Example: Equity rises to $12,000, falls to $10,800, then recovers: drawdown = $1,200 (10%).

Recovery factor

Net profit relative to the worst drawdown. Higher means profits comfortably outweigh the pain.

Recovery factor = net profit ÷ max drawdown

Example: $6,000 net profit with a $1,500 max drawdown = 4.0.

Sharpe ratio (per trade)

Average trade result divided by the variability of results. Higher means smoother, more consistent returns.

Sharpe = mean trade P&L ÷ standard deviation of trade P&L

Example: Mean $67, standard deviation $150 → 0.45.

Green-day percentage

The share of trading days that ended with a net profit – a measure of consistency.

Green days = profitable days ÷ trading days × 100

Example: 17 green days out of 30 = 57%.

Position size

How many shares, contracts or lots to trade so that hitting your stop loses exactly your planned risk.

Size = (account × risk %) ÷ (stop distance × point value)

Example: $10,000 account, 1% risk ($100), stop $2 away on a stock: 50 shares.

Discipline score

EdgeTrace’s 0–100 measure of how consistently you follow your process: 60% share of mistake-free trades plus 40% plan adherence.

Score = (clean-trade share × 0.6 + plan-followed share × 0.4) × 100

Example: 16 of 30 clean trades and plan followed on 16 of 30 → 53 ("inconsistent").

Which trading metric matters most?

Expectancy matters most, because it combines win rate and the size of wins and losses into one number: the average profit per trade. A strategy with positive expectancy makes money over enough trades; negative expectancy loses money no matter how often it wins.

Is a high win rate good in trading?

Not by itself. A 90% win rate with tiny wins and occasional huge losses loses money, while a 35% win rate with 1:3 risk:reward is profitable. Always read win rate together with average win ÷ average loss, or simply look at expectancy and profit factor.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good profit factor in trading?

A profit factor above 1.0 means a strategy is profitable. Around 1.5 is solid, 2.0 or more is excellent, and anything consistently above 3 over many trades is exceptional and worth checking for overfitting or small sample size.

How do you calculate expectancy in trading?

Expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss). For example, with a 55% win rate, $165 average win and $61 average loss: 0.55 × 165 − 0.45 × 61 = $63.30 expected per trade.

What does R-multiple mean?

R-multiple expresses a trade’s result in units of the risk you planned. If you risk $100 and make $300, that trade is +3R. Measuring in R lets you compare trades of different sizes and spot losses that exceeded your plan.
